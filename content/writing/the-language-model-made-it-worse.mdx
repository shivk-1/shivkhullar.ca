when you press a key, your forearm muscles contract, and that contraction leaks a
few millionths of a volt to the surface of your skin. electrodes on a wristband
pick it up. no needles, no implant, just a strap.

the question is whether you can run that backwards: from voltage, recover the
keystroke. do it well enough and you get a keyboard with no keyboard.

this post is about a comparison inside that problem. there are two schools of
thought on turning a noisy decode into text, and i wanted to know which wins with
everything underneath held identical. one of them is the move everybody reaches
for right now, "put a language model on it." that one lost. it lost by being
worse than doing nothing.

## the signal

**surface emg** (electromyography) is muscle electrical activity recorded from
the skin. the dataset is `emg2qwerty`, released by meta: two wristbands, 16
electrodes each, 32 channels, sampled 2,000 times per second while people type on
a normal keyboard, with the keylogger output as ground truth. 30 users, 366
sessions, 111 hours, 1.7 million keystrokes.

four seconds of it, both wrists, titled with the text being typed:

![](/writing/emg-hero.png)

_each row is one electrode. the spikes are muscle bursts, roughly keystrokes,
overlapping and smeared._

the problem is visible immediately. keystrokes are not separated events, they are
overlapping bursts, because your fingers move toward the next key before the
current one bottoms out. and which electrode lights up depends on where the
wristband sat that morning.

zoom out to 20 seconds across all 32 electrodes and the structure is clearer:

![](/writing/emg-electrodes.png)

_brighter means more muscle activity. the hands take turns and specific
electrodes fire for specific fingers. that structure is what makes this solvable
at all._

## the pipeline, in plain terms first

the standard attack has two halves.

the **encoder** looks at the signal and guesses characters. it does not know
english. it knows "this pattern of muscle activity looks like an r." the ears.

the **decoder** turns that stream of guesses into a string. this is where
knowledge about language gets injected, because the encoder's output is full of
errors and english has structure you can exploit.

the argument in this post is entirely about the second half. the encoder stays
frozen across both experiments: same ears, different brains.

### what the encoder actually is

the encoder does not eat raw voltage. it eats a **spectrogram**, a picture of
which frequencies are present at each moment in time. muscle activity has a
characteristic frequency signature, so this makes the job far easier than raw
waveform would.

![](/writing/emg-spectrogram.png)

_what the model actually sees: time left to right, frequency bottom to top,
brightness is energy. note that it stops at 250 Hz. that matters later._

from there:

- a **rotation-invariant** electrode front-end: it tries not to care if you wore
  the wristband rotated differently than yesterday
- **TDS convolution** blocks (time-depth-separable) for local patterns in time,
  the muscle-burst shapes
- a **transformer** over time, letting distant moments in the window talk to each
  other. this is the piece borrowed from brain2qwerty's meg decoder, and what the
  original emg2qwerty baseline lacks
- a linear layer to 99 classes: 98 keys plus one special symbol

24.2m parameters, trained from scratch on 4-second windows.

### what CTC is, and why it matters here

that 99th class is called **blank**, and it exists because of a real problem: the
model guesses about 125 times per second, but you typed maybe 8 characters in
that second, and nobody labeled which millisecond each keypress happened. so how
do you train against the text at all?

**CTC** (connectionist temporal classification) is the answer. the model emits
blank whenever nothing is happening, and repeats collapse: `hh_ee_lll_o` becomes
`hello`. training sums probability over every alignment that would collapse to
the right answer, so the model is never told where the characters are, only what
they were.

the consequence is the hinge of this post. at decode time CTC hands you not a
string but a probability distribution over characters at every time step, which
implies a huge weighted set of possible strings. that set is the **lattice**.
what you are allowed to do with it is the entire disagreement.

### how error gets measured

**CER**, character error rate: insertions plus deletions plus substitutions,
divided by the length of the truth. 0% is perfect, 100% means as many mistakes as
characters. **WER** is the same at word level, always worse, since one wrong
character ruins a whole word.

the reference point throughout is **MyoText**, a published system on this task
reporting 5.4% CER and 6.5% WER from hand-built physiological priors plus a
constrained search. that is the target. my encoder, decoded greedily (take the
most likely character at each step, collapse), lands at **46.4% CER**.

bad in absolute terms, unremarkable in context: emg2qwerty's own generic-user
baseline is around 55% greedy CER before its language model. 46.4% is a working
front-end, just a noisy one, and noise is the condition where the two decoding
philosophies disagree hardest.

## the two philosophies

![](/writing/emg-method.png)

_same data, same encoder weights, two decoders. one may leave the lattice, one
may not._

**unconstrained.** throw the lattice away, take the greedy string, hand it to a
language model, ask for the correct sentence. i used `byt5-small`, a
character-level model, fine-tuned on 65,676 (noisy, clean) pairs from my own
encoder's output. it can emit anything. the brain2qwerty-style move, and what
most people reach for first.

**constrained.** keep the lattice. run **CTC prefix beam search**, which carries
many candidate strings forward at once instead of committing to the best
character at each step, scoring each with a 5-gram character language model built
from the training text. **beam width** 24 is how many candidates stay alive.
**alpha** (0.4) is how loudly the language model votes against the encoder.
**beta** (0.6) is a per-character bonus offsetting the language model's bias
toward short strings. the key property: it can only reweight paths the encoder
already put probability on. it cannot invent.

both of these are "add a language model." that phrase covers two completely
different operations.

## the free language model hallucinates fluently

byt5 trained fine. training loss fell 2.23 to 0.86 over three epochs. validation
CER went **up** every epoch, 58.1% to 60.3% to 60.7%, finishing at 56.6% against
the 46.4% baseline on a matched eval subset.

![](/writing/emg-byt5-overfit.png)

_left: training loss falling while validation error climbs, textbook overfitting.
right: the encoder alone beats the encoder plus the llm._

the failure is legible in the strings:

```
noisy   quclige x ju        byt5  quickly brown fox ju     truth  the quick brown fox ju
noisy   cnea ssalae         byt5  cheapest salad           truth  evision kinda salad
noisy   epeneus             byt5  epeneus                  truth  entrepreneur
```

`cheapest salad` is real english. confident, well-formed, wrong. the noisy string
had partially-correct characters in it and the model traded them for a fluent
phrase sharing almost nothing with the target. CER punishes that trade,
correctly.

this is not byt5 being a bad model. it is a model asked to reconstruct content
that is no longer in its input. at 46% CER the message is gone, so the only thing
left to condition on is the prior, and the prior is "what does english look
like." you get english. you do not get the sentence.

## the constrained one helped every user

before decoding i also **personalized** the encoder, fine-tuning it on each
user's own sessions. anatomy and typing style differ enough that one generic
model leaves a lot on the table.

![](/writing/emg-personalization.png)

_generic against personalized, all 30 users. helps almost everyone, unevenly.
orange line is MyoText, for scale._

then the beam search on top. mean CER 42.9% to 39.3%, word error rate 93.1% to
85.7%, and it improved **all 12 users** it ran on. not most. all.

the word-level gain being roughly twice the character-level gain is the tell. the
language model is mostly fixing **boundaries**, where one word ends and the next
starts, which is exactly the call the encoder is genuinely torn on and the
lattice already holds as a near-tie. that is where a prior is information.
rewriting content is where a prior becomes fiction.

stated plainly: a language model helps a noisy decoder exactly as far as it stays
inside the set of hypotheses the signal supports. once it can leave that set, its
fluency stops being an asset and becomes the mechanism by which the error is
made.

## the lever nobody was arguing about

here is the part that made the whole comparison feel small.

every number above ran on 500 Hz data. the recordings are 2,000 Hz. i decimated
them 4x early on so the persisted tensors would fit in free-tier storage, then
built eight milestones on top of that decision without revisiting it.

there is a hard fact about sampling: you can only represent frequencies up to
half your sample rate. that is **nyquist**. at 500 Hz everything above 250 Hz is
not attenuated, it is gone. go back to the spectrogram image, the one stopping at
exactly 250 Hz. fine finger movement lives partly above that line, and i had
binned it for storage reasons.

re-persisting six users at full 2 kHz and re-running the identical
personalization recipe, no architecture change, no decoder change, nothing but
sample rate, took mean CER from 42.3% to **27.8%**. that one data step beat every
decoding idea in the project combined, including the beam search i had just spent
a milestone validating.

stack both levers and it lands at **24.8%** mean, **22.2%** for the best user.
the two effects are independent and additive, which is its own quiet confirmation
they were fixing different problems.

![](/writing/cer-journey.png)

_the whole arc. every constrained or fidelity lever moved error down. the one
unconstrained-llm lever moved it up._

```
encoder, greedy       46.4%
+ byt5 (free llm)     56.6%   worse
+ personalize (500)   42.3%
+ beam-lm (500)       39.3%
+ 2 khz, greedy       27.8%
+ 2 khz + beam-lm     24.8%
  best user           22.2%
myotext (published)    5.4%
```

## what this shows and what it doesn't

24.8% is still nearly five times MyoText's 5.4%, and i am not going to dress that
up. this is a from-scratch reproduction on a six-user subset at free-kaggle-t4
budget, the paper's exact 30-user subset was never released so mine is a
documented deviation, and the generic encoder never got retrained at 2 kHz. not a
competitive system.

what it does support is narrower, and travels further than emg:

- on a noisy front-end, **constrained decoding beats unconstrained correction**,
  and the unconstrained version can be worse than doing nothing.
- a generative corrector's confidence is not evidence. it is the delivery
  mechanism for the error.
- before tuning the decoder, audit what you threw away in preprocessing. i spent
  four milestones on decoding and one afternoon on sample rate. the afternoon won
  by four times.

that last one is what i would actually tell someone. the decimation was never a
modeling choice i defended. it was a storage workaround i stopped thinking about,
and it capped every result downstream of it for months. those are the expensive
ones. they never look like bugs. they look like settings.
