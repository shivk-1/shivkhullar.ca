reading a paper gives you the authors' account of what happened. re-implementing
it gives you the parts they left on the floor: the initialization that mattered,
the learning-rate warmup buried in an appendix, the preprocessing step mentioned
once in a footnote. that gap is the interesting part.

## the claim and the artifact are different things

a paper is a claim. the code, if it exists, is one artifact that supports it.
they diverge more often than you would expect, and usually not because anyone
was dishonest. the version that produced the numbers is rarely the version that
gets published.

so the first job is not "does this work" but "what exactly is being asserted."
write the claim down as something falsifiable before touching an editor.

## start from the ablations, not the headline

the headline result is the least informative number in the paper. it is a single
point in a space of choices, and it has survived selection pressure. the
ablations tell you which of those choices carry weight.

if the ablation table says a component buys two points, and your re-implementation
sees that component buy nothing, you have found something worth chasing. that is
a real signal, and it is far cheaper to reach than a full reproduction.

## budget for the boring failures

most of the time lost is not conceptual. it is:

- a tokenizer that silently differs
- an evaluation split that is not the split you assumed
- a metric that averages over examples where the paper averages over batches
- a seed that was never fixed

none of these are interesting, all of them will cost you a day. the fix is to
build the evaluation harness first, on known inputs with known outputs, before
any of the model code exists.

## instrument the failure, not the success

when the numbers match, you learn almost nothing. when they do not, the useful
move is to make the discrepancy legible rather than to keep tuning until it
disappears.

```python
# log the distribution, not the scalar
for name, tensor in probes.items():
    log(name, tensor.mean().item(), tensor.std().item(), tensor.abs().max().item())
```

a scalar tells you that something is wrong. a distribution tells you where.

## what you keep

the reproduction itself is disposable. what persists is the harness, the probes,
and a much sharper sense of which claims in a field are load-bearing and which
are decoration.

that is the actual return on the time, and it does not show up in whether the
final number matched.
